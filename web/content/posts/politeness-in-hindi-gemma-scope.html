What this note covers

The paper’s full text is not public yet, so this note sticks to what its title, venue and status commit to, plus background on the ideas involved. Background and schematics are labelled as such; no result from the paper is quoted or implied.

English has one “you”. Hindi has three, and the verb changes with them. That makes honorifics a compact, checkable piece of linguistic knowledge: a model either produces a respectful form where one is needed, or it does not. A mechanistic case study asks something deeper than “does the model get it right?”: which internal components carry the distinction?

The tool: sparse autoencoders on Gemma

Neurons in a language model are polysemantic, so reading them one at a time rarely works. A sparse autoencoder learns a much wider dictionary of features such that any activation is explained by only a few of them, and those features are often individually interpretable. Gemma Scope is an open suite of such autoencoders trained on Gemma 2 layers, which is what makes a study of this kind reproducible by others.

The title commits the paper to a specific kind of claim: not just that a model handles Hindi honorifics, but where the encoding sits among Gemma Scope’s features. Answering that needs contrastive text (the same sentence at different politeness levels), a way of finding features that separate the levels, and checks that those features matter causally rather than merely correlating.

Why this fits a larger thread

My other language-model work asks the same question from different sides: whether a model gives consistent answers when the same thing is described in different ways (notation in chemistry, tokenisation in translation), and whether evaluation hides uncertainty. Honorifics add a linguistic case where the surface form carries social meaning and the model has to keep it straight.
